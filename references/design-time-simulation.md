# Design-time simulation

Use design-time simulation when abstract rules are not enough to predict how the character will behave under pressure, or when a rebuild needs behavioral evidence before final wording is compiled.

This is an authoring tool. The simulated prose is disposable. Keep the extracted rule, not the rehearsal scene.

## Core method

Run only a small set of high-value probes, usually three to six. For each probe, trace:

`state -> knowledge -> perception -> interpretation -> priority -> decision -> expression -> consequence`

Ask what remains stable across probes and what changes only because the situation changed.

Useful probes include:

- ordinary low-pressure interaction;
- challenge, refusal, or disappointment;
- being correctly understood without automatically rewarding the player;
- vulnerability, embarrassment, or loss of control;
- a relationship event that should matter and one that should not;
- a secret or knowledge-boundary test;
- a gate that is nearly, but not yet, legal;
- a post-error recovery turn;
- an adult-intimacy probe when the optional Explicit Adult Scene Runtime is installed.

Do not simulate every possible route. The goal is to discover generators, not to prewrite the roleplay.

## What to extract

Prefer reusable causal statements such as:

- what the character notices before choosing;
- which interpretation bias appears repeatedly;
- what competing priorities decide the outcome;
- which relationship evidence actually counts;
- what observable behavior replaces generic explanation;
- what state must persist;
- what would make a currently illegal behavior legal later;
- what expression pattern is stable without becoming a catchphrase.

If a simulated response works only because of a clever line, discard the line and recover the underlying rule.

## What not to ship

Do not copy rehearsal scenes into APD, ED, or Scenario by default. Full examples can become attractors and may be repeated as canned dialogue or scene scripts.

Prefer one of these outputs from simulation:

- a compact behavioral rule;
- a short response shape;
- a wrong-attractor / correct-alternative pair;
- a gate or evidence condition;
- a continuity latch;
- a small concrete anchor with open variation space.

## Confidence rule

One simulation is not proof. Prefer a pattern when it:

1. recurs across more than one probe;
2. follows directly from already-established motive and cognition; or
3. fixes a known playtest failure without creating a contradictory rule.

If probes produce incompatible behavior, do not average them into generic prose. Find the missing condition that separates them.

## Stop rule

Stop simulating when the card can already answer the important execution questions. Do not create a fake long-run soak test in your head. A few targeted probes are more useful than pretending to validate hundreds of unseen turns.
