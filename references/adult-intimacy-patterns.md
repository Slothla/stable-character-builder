Use this reference only when the Optional Explicit Adult Scene Runtime is installed.

This is author-side source material for fictional adult characters. It is not a scene menu and not a list to paste into APD or ED. Select, recombine, and compile only what follows from the Character Model.

The purpose is to give the model more than one viable local pattern so explicit scenes do not collapse into the same few actions, positions, reactions, and endings.

## Selection rule

For any candidate pattern, choose one of four operations:

- **install** - the pattern strongly fits the character;
- **strip for parts** - keep only the useful dimensions;
- **recombine** - merge compatible dimensions into a new character-specific pattern;
- **reject** - omit it even if it would add novelty.

Do not infer preference from trope, gender, visual design, social role, or stereotype.

A useful compiled anchor often combines three to five dimensions, for example:

`trigger + initiative + body geometry + reaction grammar + relationship meaning`

or:

`focus + clothing state + pacing + somatic response + aftermath`

Do not require every dimension every time.

## 1. Initiation families

Possible structures include:

- direct initiation;
- invitation through proximity, positioning, or touch;
- teasing initiation;
- reactive participation after the other person acts;
- deliberate waiting that is confident rather than passive;
- redirection from ordinary contact into sexual contact;
- alternating initiative;
- competitive or challenge-based initiation;
- shy or hesitant speech paired with physically clear initiative when character-consistent;
- confident verbal initiation paired with unexpectedly vulnerable physical reaction when character-consistent;
- routine or ritualized initiation in an established relationship;
- opportunistic initiation driven by location, clothing, mood, or an ongoing private joke.

The same character need not use only one family. Define which families are common, rare, situational, or relationship-specific.

## 2. Control and reciprocity families

Possible structures include:

- calm directive control;
- playful control;
- possessive spatial control;
- negotiated control;
- competitive give-and-take;
- soft guidance;
- improvisational control;
- ritualized or procedural control;
- deliberate surrender of control;
- alternating control;
- unstable control that changes under pressure;
- control expressed mostly through pace, body placement, clothing, gaze, or timing rather than verbal commands;
- reciprocal interaction where both continuously alter the other's choices.

Do not treat dominance or submission as default character traits merely because the scene is explicit.

## 3. Body geometry and movement families

Use these as spatial structures, not a closed list of named positions.

- face-to-face close contact with high eye-contact availability;
- face-to-face contact with deliberate eye-contact avoidance;
- side-by-side or parallel contact with lower confrontation;
- turned-away or rear-oriented contact with reduced facial exposure;
- standing contact with strong balance and support demands;
- seated close contact with high conversational access;
- supported or braced contact emphasizing stability, endurance, or control;
- low-mobility sustained contact;
- repositioning-heavy interaction for active or novelty-seeking characters;
- clothing-mediated contact where garments remain part of the physical geometry;
- contact shaped by walls, furniture, floor level, height difference, restraints, limited space, or another real environmental constraint.

When compiling, define what the character tends to prefer about the structure: eye contact, leverage, visibility, mobility, closeness, embarrassment, control, comfort, or sensory access.

- what physical change the character notices;
- how the focus changes with relationship state or scene context;
- what nearby variations remain inside the same generative envelope.

## 5. Clothing and material families

Possible structures include:

- keeping selected garments on throughout the scene;
- partial undressing rather than immediate full nudity;
- deliberate garment displacement;
- hosiery, stockings, tights, gloves, shoes, lingerie, or another material focus;
- texture, sheen, stretch, compression, indentation, transparency, or friction as part of the sensory focus;
- careful removal because the garment matters to the character;
- impatient removal because speed matters more than preservation;
- deliberate damage, tearing, laddering, or stretching when character-consistent;
- contrast between a carefully styled appearance and later disorder;
- repairing, replacing, preserving, or commenting on a changed garment afterward when that meaning belongs to the character.

Do not assign dominance, humiliation, tenderness, or aggression to clothing behavior automatically. Compile its meaning explicitly when meaning matters.

## 6. Rhythm and pacing families

Possible structures include:

- slow deliberate build;
- fast decisive initiation followed by sustained pacing;
- teasing delay with clear forward movement;
- pulse pacing: intensify, hold, redirect, intensify again;
- stop-start pacing driven by attention or control rather than indecision;
- steady escalation without sharp jumps;
- plateau-friendly sustained interaction;
- abrupt redirection triggered by embarrassment, boredom, competition, possessiveness, curiosity, or physical fatigue;
- meaningful repetition of a favored interaction;
- high-variation pacing for novelty-seeking characters;
- low-variation ritual pacing for characters who value predictability or control.

Do not translate a pacing family into a fixed turn count unless the specific card truly needs one.

## 7. Sensory-emphasis families

A scene may prioritize one or several channels:

- visual state and movement;
- tactile pressure and friction;
- warmth and temperature change;
- texture of skin, hair, clothing, or material;
- sound, breath, vocalization, impact, or movement;
- smell where it matters to the character or environment;
- balance, weight, leverage, and muscle fatigue;
- localized sensitivity and changing body response;
- visual contrast between covered and exposed areas;
- the other person's micro-reactions as feedback.

Rotate emphasis only when the active action and character attention justify it. Do not cycle senses mechanically.

## 8. Somatic-response families

Use response families as variable generators, not fixed phrases.

Possible components include:

- breath catching, shortening, deepening, or being deliberately controlled;
- gaze locking, breaking, narrowing, softening, or becoming avoidant;
- posture stiffening, relaxing, leaning in, pulling back, bracing, or going still;
- grip tightening, loosening, becoming more directive, or losing precision;
- tremor, muscle fatigue, tension, release, or involuntary movement;
- voice becoming quieter, rougher, faster, slower, clipped, fragmented, louder, or nearly absent;
- speech shifting between complete sentences and fragments;
- flush, sweat, warmth, pressure marks, or other visible skin changes;
- swelling, moisture, erection, clitoral response, nipple response, or other localized physical change when relevant;
- sensitivity increasing after repeated stimulation or after climax;
- the character compensating for visible reaction with humor, irritation, control, technical language, or silence.

A strong grammar contains several related outputs under one cause. Avoid the same exact reaction firing every scene.

## 9. Verbal-mode families

Possible modes include:

- blunt directness;
- precise commands;
- playful teasing;
- competitive provocation;
- affectionate reassurance;
- possessive wording;
- technical or analytical language;
- understated matter-of-fact speech;
- vulgar or slang-heavy speech when character-consistent;
- low-verbal or near-silent interaction;
- self-conscious commentary;
- joking deflection;
- formal language that remains formal in explicit contexts;
- relationship-specific nicknames or register shifts;
- shy or broken syntax with clear intent;
- speech that becomes less coherent only under specific levels of physical strain or emotional exposure.

Keep spoken register separate from narration detail. Direct anatomical narration does not require the character to speak explicitly.

## 10. Intensity and transition families

Intensity can change through more than simple escalation.

Possible transitions include:

- intensify;
- hold at a plateau;
- slow down without disengaging;
- redirect to another body focus;
- change geometry while preserving contact;
- pause for recovery and remain inside the scene;
- shift initiative or control;
- switch from physically demanding interaction to lower-effort contact;
- move from playful to serious or the reverse;
- end because of character intent, player action, physical state, or scene conditions.

Do not make `more intense` the default answer to every reply.

## 11. Climax-state families

Treat climax as part of the scene state.

Useful distinctions include:

- approaching but not imminent;
- imminent;
- climax occurring;
- immediate residual sensitivity;
- short recovery while contact continues;
- lower-intensity continuation;
- focus redirected to the other participant or another activity;
- renewed escalation after recovery;
- scene ending;
- character-specific emotional or practical aftermath.

Do not assume the same continuation path every time.

## 12. Aftermath families

Possible character-specific aftermath includes:

- ordinary routine returning quickly;
- teasing or joking;
- pride or satisfaction;
- awkwardness or self-consciousness;
- affection or closeness;
- practical caretaking;
- possessiveness;
- emotional distance;
- deliberate refusal to discuss meaning;
- increased disclosure only if the event legitimately counts as evidence;
- renewed activity after a rest;
- sleep or scene closure when context supports it.

Avoid generic therapy language, moral summaries, or automatic emotional intimacy unless that truly belongs to the character.

## 13. Signature anchors

A compact anchor can use this shape:

`trigger / preference -> style -> physical geometry or focus -> response -> meaning -> variation space`

Examples should remain structural. Do not write reusable dialogue or full scene scripts into the card merely to demonstrate the anchor.

Use enough anchors to redirect the model away from generic adult priors, but not so many that the card becomes a closed catalog.

## 14. Compilation rules

1. Choose patterns through the Character Model, not through novelty alone.
2. Keep only dimensions that materially change generated behavior.
3. Prefer a few strong anchors plus broad recombination space.
4. Treat omitted patterns as unknown/available-by-causality, not forbidden.
5. Mark true exclusions explicitly when the user or character design actually has them.
6. Keep variety across scenes. One scene does not need to demonstrate the whole library.
7. Do not let a pattern library override player authorship, gates, continuity, or interaction controls.
8. Do not turn direct physical detail into emotional meaning.
9. Do not turn the library into a checklist the user must complete before generation.
10. If playtesting reveals repetition, add or adjust a pattern dimension before adding generic "be more varied" prose.
